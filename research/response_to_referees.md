## Response to Referee 1 (Econometrics & Identification)

### Point 1.1: Granger Causality vs. Polling Latency
> *Concern*: Standard polling takes 3–5 days of fieldwork and 1–2 days of post-stratification weighting. Could the 5–7 day Granger causality simply reflect fast market price discovery rather than true causal reflexivity?

**Response**:  
This is a fundamental identification question that lies at the core of our empirical architecture. We provide three decisive arguments and tests demonstrating that our results reflect structural reflexivity rather than mere measurement latency:

1. **Latency Profile vs. Information Lead**:  
   If prediction markets were merely aggregating existing private sentiment that pollsters take 5 days to publish, then under classical information aggregation, controlling for observable common news events (debates, speeches, policy announcements) should substantially diminish or eliminate the leading relationship. In Table 5 (Specification 1), we control for all major discrete campaign shocks in a lag-augmented VAR(8). The Wald statistic remains exceptionally large ($\chi^2 = 59.61, p < 0.0001$), demonstrating that market prices lead polls even when news events are held constant.
2. **Dynamic Equilibrium Error-Correction (VECM)**:  
   A pure measurement-lag model does not predict cointegration with directional error-correction. In Section 6.3 and Table 3, the Johansen trace test confirms cointegration between Polymarket prices and polling margins ($r=1$). The error-correction adjustment speed in the polling equation is negative and highly statistically significant ($\hat{\alpha}_{poll} = -0.0003, t = -3.20, p < 0.001$), while the price equation adjustment speed is small. Polling margins actively and continuously correct back toward the market price.
3. **The Competitiveness Multiplier as a Falsification Test**:  
   A pure measurement-delay model implies that latency is governed by survey logistics (phone interview turnaround, sample size) which do not vary systematically with state competitiveness. In contrast, our Proposition 2 microfoundation explicitly predicts that reflexivity is maximized in toss-up races ($P \approx 0.5$) because the normal density $\phi(\cdot)$ is maximized at the median voter margin. In Table 4, the interaction term between price shocks and competitiveness is positive and highly significant ($\hat{\beta}_{inter} = 1.8339, p = 0.001$). This non-linear state-level interaction cannot be generated by polling production delays.

### Point 1.2: Common Campaign News Shocks
> *Concern*: Could high-profile events (debates, assassination attempt, candidate exits) generate simultaneous responses that appear as market-led Granger causality?

**Response**:  
We have directly addressed this concern by executing an extensive event-control robustness test, reported in Table 5 (Specification 1) and discussed in Section 6.6 of the revised manuscript. We augmented the Toda-Yamamoto system with dummy indicators for:
- June 27, 2024: First Presidential Debate (Biden vs. Trump)
- July 13, 2024: Butler, PA Assassination Attempt
- July 21, 2024: President Biden Withdrawal and Harris Endorsement
- September 10, 2024: Second Presidential Debate (Harris vs. Trump)
- October 5–31, 2024: The Polymarket Whale Accumulation Surge

Even after conditioning out the variance attributable to these discrete political milestones, Polymarket prices continue to Granger-cause 538 polling margins ($\chi^2 = 59.61, p = 0.0000$). The transmission from market prices to polling numbers is continuous and persists across quiet campaign periods.

### Point 1.3: Inference with Small Cluster Counts ($G=7$)
> *Concern*: Clustering standard errors at the state level with only 7 battleground states violates asymptotic guarantees (Cameron, Gelbach, and Miller 2008), potentially causing over-rejection.

**Response**:  
We thank the referee for emphasizing cluster inference rigor. We conducted the Cameron, Gelbach, and Miller (2008) Wild Cluster Bootstrap using Webb (1999) 6-point distribution weights, specifically tailored for small cluster numbers ($G \le 10$).  
The bootstrap $p$-value for the competitiveness interaction coefficient $\hat{\beta}_{inter}$ is:
$$p_{\text{boot}} = 0.006$$
The null hypothesis $\beta_{inter} = 0$ is rejected at the 1% significance level under the wild cluster bootstrap, confirming that our inference is entirely robust to small-cluster finite-sample distortions. We have noted this in Section 6.6 of the manuscript and cited Cameron et al. (2008).

## Response to Referee 2 (Economic Mechanisms & Theory)

### Point 2.1: Mapping to Bond, Edmans, and Goldstein (2012)
> *Concern*: Differentiate whether the mechanism is "managerial learning" or "contracting/resource feedback."

**Response**:  
We have clarified this distinction in Section 3 and Section 7.1. In the taxonomy of Bond, Edmans, and Goldstein (2012), our microfoundation is primarily a **contracting and resource feedback** mechanism rather than managerial learning.  
Specifically:
- Campaign donors and Super PAC contributors face severe coordination frictions: they wish to back a viable candidate to secure future policy access or prevent an ideological opponent from winning, but refuse to waste funds on doomed candidacies.
- Prediction market prices serve as a high-frequency, publicly verifiable coordination equilibrium. A rising $P_t$ loosens the candidate's fundraising constraint $\kappa P_t$, unlocking advertising expenditure, ground-game voter contact, and paid digital mobilization that directly shift the terminal distribution of votes $\theta_T$.
- Simultaneously, earned media coverage operates via salience cascades (Morris and Shin 2002): journalists cite market probabilities as objective evidence of momentum, inducing an informational cascade among low-information voters.

### Point 2.2: Limits to Arbitrage in Reflexive Markets
> *Concern*: What prevents rational arbitrageurs from trading against the reflexivity multiplier?

**Response**:  
In standard asset pricing theory (Grossman and Stiglitz 1980), arbitrageurs exploit deviations of price from fundamental value because the fundamental liquidation value is exogenous.  
In a reflexive prediction market, however, **the liquidation payoff $Y = \mathbf{1}\{\theta_T \ge 0\}$ is endogenously determined by the market price trajectory $\{P_t\}$**.  
Consider an arbitrageur who observes Trump at 60% on Polymarket while current polls stand at 50%. In a non-reflexive market, the arbitrageur would short Trump contracts. But in our model:
$$\theta_T = \rho \theta_t + \lambda \kappa P_t + \eta_{t+1}$$
Because $P_t = 0.60$ induces donor capital inflows and positive media momentum ($\lambda \kappa > 0$), the candidate's actual probability of victory increases toward 60%. An arbitrageur who attempts to short an elevated price will suffer large expected losses because the price itself validates the high probability. This establishes a theoretical **limit to arbitrage unique to reflexive systems**: self-fulfilling price movements destroy fundamental arbitrage.

## Response to Referee 3 (Market Microstructure & Data)

### Point 3.1: The October Whale Trader (Théo) Influx
> *Concern*: Could the entire empirical result be driven by the October 2024 whale trader who wagered >\$30M on Trump?

**Response**:  
We have executed a clean subsample stability analysis, reported in Table 5 (Specification 2) and discussed in Section 6.6.  
We partitioned the national dataset into:
1. **Pre-Whale Sample** (March 1, 2024 to October 4, 2024, $T=218$ days): Prior to the emergence of the French national's multi-account accumulation on Polymarket.
2. **Full Sample** (March 1, 2024 to November 5, 2024, $T=251$ days).

In the pre-whale sample, the Toda-Yamamoto Wald statistic for Polymarket Granger-causing polling margins is:
$$\chi^2 = 61.09 \quad (p = 0.0000)$$
The statistical significance and magnitude of the reflexivity feedback are actually slightly stronger in the pre-whale period than in the full sample. This definitively refutes the hypothesis that our findings are an artifact of the late-cycle whale accumulation. Rather, political reflexivity operated continuously throughout the 2024 presidential election.

### Point 3.2: 538 Polling Aggregation and Day-of-Week Seasonality
> *Concern*: Does forward-filling and smoothing in 538 polling data introduce artificial persistence or day-of-week release bias?

**Response**:  
We addressed this via two distinct empirical safeguards:
1. **Toda-Yamamoto Lag Augmentation**:  
   The Toda-Yamamoto (1995) procedure fits a $\text{VAR}(k + d_{\max})$ where $d_{\max} = 1$ is the maximum integration order. By testing hypotheses solely on the first $k$ parameter matrices while letting the extra lag soak up non-stationary and MA smoothing dynamics, the Wald statistic converges to a standard asymptotic $\chi^2(k)$ distribution, free from pre-test bias.
2. **Day-of-Week Fixed Effects**:  
   In Table 5 (Specification 3), we added day-of-week fixed effects ($DOW \in \{Mon, \dots, Sun\}$) to the battleground state panel model. The interaction coefficient remains virtually unchanged ($\hat{\beta}_{inter} = 1.8820, p = 0.0005$ vs. baseline $1.8339$), demonstrating that polling release schedules do not confound our estimates.

### Point 3.3: Third-Party Exit (RFK Jr.) and Contract Boundaries
> *Concern*: Did RFK Jr.'s presence and subsequent exit on August 23, 2024 distort the margin metric?

**Response**:  
Throughout our analysis, we used the two-party national winner market ($P_t^{Rep} - P_t^{Dem}$) as well as candidate winner contracts. Because Polymarket winner contracts are binary options settling at \$1.00 for the winner and \$0.00 otherwise, the two-party margin directly captures the probability differential. Structural break Chow tests conducted around August 23, 2024 indicate no significant shift in the cointegrating vector ($F = 0.84, p = 0.47$).
